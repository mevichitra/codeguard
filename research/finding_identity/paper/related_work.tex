\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\textit{Tracking warnings across versions.} Spacco, Hovemeyer and Pugh added two warning trackers to FindBugs, so that reviewers could remember warnings already judged safe~\cite{spacco2006}. Later work used tracking as a means to another end: Avgustinov et al.\ followed violations through revision histories at Semmle to credit each developer with the violations they introduced or removed~\cite{avgustinov2015}, and Kim and Ernst mined warning histories to rank warning categories by how often fixes removed them~\cite{kim2007}. In both, the tracker is part of the method, and its errors reach the results unmeasured.

The closest work is by Li and Yang~\cite{li2022statictracker}. They labelled the evolution of 3,451 SpotBugs and PMD warnings in four Java projects by hand, showed that an existing tracker failed on refactorings, code shifts and volatile metadata, and proposed StaticTracker, which raised tracking precision from 64.4\% to 90.3\%. We do not build a better tracker; we compare the methods that shipped tools use, ported from their source, on the same findings, and we count the two kinds of error separately, since a baseline that hides new findings is a security problem and one that reports old findings as new is only a nuisance.

\textit{Where tools need identity.} Developers name false positives and the way warnings are presented as barriers to static analysis~\cite{johnson2013}, and at Google the analyses developers act on report in code review~\cite{sadowski2015tricorder,sadowski2018lessons}: showing only the findings a change introduced needs exactly the identity we measure. Suppressions depend on it too: Hu et al.\ found that half of the suppression comments in their study suppress no warning at all~\cite{hu2025suppressed}. SARIF~2.1.0 gives tools fields for fingerprints and for a result's baseline state~\cite{sarif2020}, yet the tools we compare match results in very different ways (Section~\ref{sec:schemes}).
